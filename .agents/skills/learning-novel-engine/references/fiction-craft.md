## Draft from a viewpoint

Choose who owns the scene and write from the limits of that consciousness.

Track:

```text
what the character notices first
what they habitually measure or compare
what they misunderstand
what they refuse to notice
what vocabulary belongs to them
what bodily or environmental signals reach attention under pressure
```

The same laboratory should look different to a hardware engineer, a signal-processing researcher, a manager, and a frightened beginner.

Do not report another character's inner state from a limited viewpoint. Render it through speech, timing, posture, action, and the viewpoint character's possibly wrong interpretation.

## Control psychic distance

Move deliberately between:

- distant summary for transitions, elapsed time, and low-value logistics;
- medium distance for clear action and orientation;
- close distance for decisions, shame, fear, desire, inference, and conceptual change.

Default model prose often stays at a safe middle distance. At important hinges, let narration inherit the character's vocabulary, associations, and judgment rather than announcing “he thought” or “she realized” from outside.

Do not remain maximally close during every calculation or action. Distance variation gives shape.

## Give every paragraph a job

A paragraph may perform several functions:

```text
advance action
change interpretation
reveal character
alter relationship
ground place
deliver evidence
compress time
create or discharge tension
```

Delete or merge a paragraph that only repeats a conclusion, labels a feeling already visible, or explains why the previous paragraph mattered.

Do not write a paragraph merely because an outline bullet exists.

## Use sensory detail through attention

Choose one or two concrete details that the viewpoint character would notice now.

Prefer details that also reveal:

- expertise;
- emotional state;
- danger;
- social hierarchy;
- technical evidence;
- recurring motif;
- changed environment.

Avoid generic inventories of rain, coffee, fluorescent light, screens, and silence unless those details perform specific work. A place becomes memorable through selective attention, not five-sense cataloguing.

## Write interiority as active processing

Interiority may contain:

- a prediction;
- a comparison with prior experience;
- a rehearsal of what to say;
- an intrusive association;
- an attempted rationalization;
- a sudden change in what evidence means;
- a conflict between what the character knows and what they want to believe.

Avoid polished essays about the character's own feelings. Under pressure, thought may fragment, loop, skip, or seize on an irrelevant detail.

A conceptual insight should alter the character's perception of the same object. Show the object becoming legible in a new way.

## Make dialogue multi-purpose

Dialogue should do at least two things at once:

```text
pursue a practical goal
reveal or conceal motive
alter status
test trust
deliver partial information
force commitment
create misunderstanding
```

Characters rarely answer the exact question cleanly. They may:

- deflect;
- answer a narrower question;
- over-explain to protect status;
- use jargon as a shield;
- joke;
- interrupt;
- wait too long;
- attack the premise;
- agree publicly and resist privately.

Use clean explanatory dialogue only when the relationship and situation make that behavior plausible.

Break long explanations with actions, objections, evidence, or changing stakes. An action beat should not be random choreography. It should reveal attention, pressure, status, or withheld emotion.

## Differentiate voices

For each recurring character, define:

```text
sentence length tendency
preferred vocabulary
metaphor source
certainty style
question style
humor or deflection
stress deformation
what they never say directly
```

Voice is not a catchphrase. It is a pattern of selection.

Test voice by removing dialogue tags from a short exchange. If every speaker remains equally articulate, balanced, and emotionally literate, strengthen differentiation.

Narration in close third should also inherit the viewpoint character's lexical and evaluative habits.

## Shape sentence and paragraph rhythm

Match rhythm to cognitive and dramatic action.

Use shorter units for:

- impact;
- interruption;
- commitment;
- reversal;
- recognition;
- danger.

Use longer cumulative units for:

- observation;
- reasoning;
- layered memory;
- ambivalence;
- slow environmental pressure.

Avoid assigning one rhythm to the entire manuscript. Technical content does not require uniformly long sentences. Tension does not require endless fragments.

Paragraph lengths should respond to beats rather than forming a regular staircase.

## Enter scenes late and leave on consequence

Begin when something is already being attempted, resisted, noticed, or decided. Orient through action unless the setting itself is the event.

Leave after:

- a decision changes the available future;
- evidence makes an old belief untenable;
- a relationship shifts;
- a cost becomes visible;
- a new question emerges from the result.

Do not append an explanatory moral, chapter summary, or decorative ominous sentence after the real ending has occurred.

## Preserve subtext and reader work

Trust the reader to infer:

- emotion from behavior;
- motive from inconsistency;
- technical significance from repeated evidence;
- relationship history from what characters avoid;
- the next diagnostic step from live alternatives.

Clarify the object, stakes, and causal relations. Do not clarify every implication.

Before explaining, ask:

```text
Can the reader infer this from action, evidence, or contrast?
Will omission create productive inference or merely confusion?
```

## Avoid common model defaults

Revise when prose shows sustained patterns such as:

- mentor question followed by polished answer followed by summary;
- characters naming the lesson;
- every paragraph having similar length and cadence;
- generic atmosphere that could belong to any story;
- emotion labels followed by stock body language;
- exact symmetry between speakers;
- repeated “not X but Y” contrasts;
- rhetorical summaries after the scene has shown the point;
- grand statements attached to routine technical facts;
- chapter endings that advertise the next topic;
- interchangeable competent prose with no viewpoint fingerprint.

Do not ban a construction mechanically. Repair the missing intention, specificity, or variation.

## Line revision sequence

Revise from large to small:

1. **scene necessity**: does the scene alter story and knowledge state?
2. **beat order**: does pressure cause evidence, decision, and consequence?
3. **viewpoint**: is attention character-specific and limited?
4. **information**: what can be withheld, moved, or embodied?
5. **dialogue and interiority**: do they reveal conflict and reasoning?
6. **paragraph rhythm**: do lengths and turns follow beats?
7. **sentence and word choice**: remove generic phrasing and accidental repetition.
8. **ending**: stop at the causal consequence.

A beautiful sentence cannot rescue a scene without agency or necessity.

## Small prose audit

Mark any sentence that:

- could move to another novel after changing one technical noun;
- tells the reader what to feel after behavior already showed it;
- explains a conclusion before evidence earns it;
- uses a sensory detail with no viewpoint or scene function;
- gives every character the same reasoning style;
- summarizes rather than advances.

Repair with a more specific action, perception, omission, contradiction, or consequence. Do not merely replace one adjective with another.
