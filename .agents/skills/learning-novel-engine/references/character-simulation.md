## Continuity is not simulation

A continuity ledger can state that a character knows OFDM synchronization, distrusts management, and is injured. It cannot by itself predict how that person speaks, notices, bargains, or fails.

Maintain two layers:

```text
canonical state: facts that must remain true
behavioral model: tendencies that generate plausible action
```

Canonical state is binding. Behavioral tendencies are hypotheses that may evolve through the manuscript.

## Character fingerprint

For each important character, record:

```text
external want
private want
fear or wound
misbelief or protected belief
domain competence
domain blind spots
evidence they trust
reasoning habit
attention bias
status strategy
conflict strategy
stress deformation
speech rhythm
preferred vocabulary
metaphor source
humor or deflection
what they do instead of naming emotion
what they will not say directly
relationship-specific behavior
```

Keep the fingerprint concrete. “Smart, kind, introverted” produces little behavior. “Trusts instrument traces more than verbal assurances, becomes pedantic when status is threatened, and avoids apologizing by fixing equipment” produces scenes.

## Pressure simulation

Before drafting a pivotal scene, privately simulate the character through four questions:

1. What do they want before the lesson exists?
2. What action would their current model and personality produce?
3. What evidence would they resist, misread, or notice first?
4. What would accepting the new model cost them socially or emotionally?

The technical answer may be easy while acceptance is hard. A character may know a formula and still refuse its implication because it exposes a mistake, betrays an ally, or threatens identity.

## Relationship vector

Voice and behavior change by relationship. For each active pair, track:

```text
power balance
trust
debt
resentment
admiration
dependency
unspoken topic
usual conversational move
what each wants from the other now
```

The same engineer may explain patiently to a junior, compete with a peer, conceal uncertainty from a manager, and speak in fragments to a close friend.

Do not let technical dialogue float free of relationship.

## Agency test

A viewpoint character has agency when they:

- choose what to inspect;
- form or reject a hypothesis;
- commit to an action;
- select among costs;
- change another person's options;
- bear consequences;
- revise a goal;
- withhold or reveal information.

Receiving an explanation is not agency. Repeating it is not agency. Agreeing with the mentor is not agency.

For each scene, identify:

```text
decision owned by the viewpoint character
information only they contribute
cost they accept or avoid
state that changes because of their action
```

A learner may need help without surrendering causal ownership.

## Mentor and expert design

Avoid one omniscient curriculum carrier. Distribute expertise across:

- people with different specialties;
- instruments with different failure modes;
- field evidence;
- code and documentation;
- remembered experience;
- competing institutions;
- characters whose incentives distort what they say.

Give experts:

```text
a goal unrelated to teaching
a domain boundary
a cost for being wrong
a social strategy
a reason to simplify, delay, conceal, or overstate
```

A good mentor can design a test without naming the conclusion. They can also be impatient, mistaken, protective, political, or unable to translate their intuition.

## In-character interrogation

Use a short private dialogue to discover voice or pressure-test a choice. Supply only what the character knows at that moment.

Ask questions such as:

- What do you think is failing?
- What observation would embarrass your current diagnosis?
- Why are you not telling the other person?
- What would make you change your mind?
- What do you notice in the room that nobody else does?
- What are you trying to win besides the technical argument?

Let the character be evasive or inconsistent. Do not force a clean self-analysis. Extract useful behavioral evidence afterward, then leave role-play mode.

## Voice deformation under stress

Do not keep a character's speech mechanically constant. Stress may cause:

- shorter clauses;
- excessive precision;
- repeated questions;
- inappropriate humor;
- formal language;
- silence;
- topic changes;
- attacks on definitions;
- rushed agreement;
- over-detailed explanation.

Define the character's normal register and one or two stress deformations. Use them selectively.

## Learning without personality replacement

A knowledge update should change what a character can predict or do, not replace their temperament.

After learning, the character may:

- use the concept for a self-serving goal;
- understand the mechanism but dislike the conclusion;
- apply it competently while explaining it badly;
- transfer it through a personal analogy;
- overgeneralize it;
- conceal it;
- weaponize it in an argument;
- notice a new uncertainty.

Do not turn every learner into a calm miniature teacher.

## Character swap test

Take a key scene and imagine another major character in the same role.

If the following remain unchanged, the original scene is under-characterized:

- first noticed evidence;
- hypothesis;
- wording;
- emotional cost;
- chosen test;
- willingness to admit error;
- resulting relationship shift.

Rewrite the scene packet before line editing.

## State update

After an accepted scene, update only durable consequences:

```text
new knowledge
new belief or doubt
changed relationship
new debt or promise
injury, resource, location, or status change
new behavioral evidence
```

Do not turn a one-time stressed reaction into a permanent trait without later support.
